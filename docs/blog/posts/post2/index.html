Same Goals, Different Results: What Scoring Misses in the NHL

web-scraping
sports
data-analysis
Author

Rui Bao

Published

September 21, 2026

Two NHL teams scored 235 goals each, yet one won 48 games and the other only 28. In the records labeled 2006, the Minnesota Wild and Washington Capitals looked identical on scoring totals. Their results were far apart.

The difference becomes easier to understand when goals allowed enter the picture. Minnesota conceded 191 goals; Washington conceded 286. That comparison raises a practical question: when reviewing a team’s season, how much do we miss by looking at offense alone?

I scraped 582 team-season records from Scrape This Site’s hockey pages. In this sample, goal differential—goals scored minus goals allowed—has a much stronger relationship with winning percentage than scoring alone.

Minnesota’s goal differential was +44; Washington’s was -51. The winning percentages were 58.5% and 34.1%, respectively. A ranking based only on goals scored would treat them as equals, missing a substantial difference in their records.

I selected the same-year, equal-scoring pair with the largest winning-percentage gap in the sample. It is a deliberately clear illustration, not a typical pair or proof that goals allowed caused the entire difference. The broader comparison below uses all the records.

Scoring matters, but leaves plenty unexplained

Goals scored and winning percentage have a correlation of 0.48. The upward pattern makes sense, but the spread matters too: similar scoring totals occur alongside quite different winning percentages. For a quick review, I would treat goals scored as one piece of information rather than a complete assessment of performance.

Adding goals allowed brings the pattern into focus

For goal differential, the correlation rises to 0.89. Teams that outscored their opponents more heavily generally won a larger share of games in this sample.

This is not a surprising new law of hockey: scoring and conceding are directly connected to game outcomes. The useful finding is how much tighter the relationship becomes when both sides enter the comparison. It supports using goal differential as a first summary of a completed season, then checking offense and goals allowed separately.

What I would do with this comparison

If I were comparing two teams, I would first compare their goal differentials within the same season. I would then look at goals scored and allowed to see what lies behind the difference. The Minnesota–Washington example shows why stopping at scoring totals can be misleading.

I would not use these correlations alone to predict next season or recommend a roster change. All measures describe the same completed seasons. The data pool different schedules and scoring environments, and total goals are not per-game rates. The teaching-site records have not been independently verified against official NHL statistics.

The takeaway is straightforward: before calling a team strong because it scores a lot, check what it gives up. That is a more useful way to read these results than treating the larger correlation as a forecasting model.

Data, methods and reproducibility

I used rvest to collect the first 24 pages of a public scraping practice site. This is a subset, not a claim to cover every available record. New requests are spaced one second apart; saved HTML pages are reused on later renders. No login, CAPTCHA or paywall is bypassed.

After numeric conversion and removal of 0 incomplete rows, the sample contains 582 team-seasons with year labels 1990–2011. I check duplicate team-year records and value ranges, retain the site’s reported winning percentage, and calculate Pearson correlations. Retrieval dates and source URLs are saved with the data.

The repository contains the full R code and replication README. Source snapshots and raw data are in data/raw, cleaned data in data/clean, charts in figures, and correlations and the example-selection results in results.

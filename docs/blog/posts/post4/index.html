Eating Out vs. Eating In: Which Prices Rose Faster?

Author

Rui Bao

Published

October 4, 2026

Eating out got more expensive faster than buying groceries over the decade from January 2015 to December 2024. U.S. prices for food away from home rose 48.1%, compared with 26.8% for food at home. Yet groceries experienced the higher peak in annual inflation.

Those findings answer two different questions: how much have prices risen, and when were they rising fastest? The distinction matters for anyone trying to understand why food still feels expensive after inflation slows.

I compare monthly food price indexes from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, retrieved through FRED. These are national urban averages for a fixed historical period, not today’s menu or supermarket prices.

Eating out pulled further ahead

Both lines begin at 100 in January 2015, making their percentage changes comparable. By December 2024, the cumulative increase for eating out exceeded that for groceries by 21.2 percentage points.

The gap was already developing before the pandemic. Grocery prices stayed relatively close to their starting level for several years, while food-away-from-home prices kept rising. Looking only at the later grocery surge would miss that earlier divergence.

For me, the useful distinction is between a smaller price increase and a cheaper meal. This chart establishes the first, not the second. It cannot tell us how many dollars someone saves by cooking the same dinner at home. That would require actual meal prices, and a broader comparison would include cooking time.

Groceries had the sharper inflation peak

Grocery inflation peaked at 13.5% in August 2022, compared with a peak of 8.8% in March 2023 for eating out. Groceries had the more intense annual increase even though they rose less over the full decade.

That is why I would read these two charts together. The first shows the accumulated increase; the second reveals when the pressure intensified. Neither alone gives the full picture of how food prices changed.

By December 2024, annual inflation had slowed to 1.8% for groceries and 3.6% for eating out. This meant prices were above their levels a year earlier, but increasing more slowly than at the peaks. It did not undo the previous increases. Lower inflation and an expensive grocery bill can coexist.

Your shopping basket matters

Even within groceries, the increases differed. Nonalcoholic beverages rose 30.9%, compared with 18.4% for fruits and vegetables. A single grocery average hides that variation.

For a household budget, I would look at frequently purchased categories as well as the headline index. Someone’s exposure depends on how much of their spending goes to each category. These bars do not measure any particular household’s inflation, and the largest percentage increase need not make the largest contribution to its bill.

A smaller increase also does not make a category cheaper in dollar terms. The chart gives a reason to examine the shopping basket, not a shopping list of what to substitute.

What the comparison tells us

Eating out had the larger cumulative increase, while groceries had the sharper inflation peak. The answer to “which rose faster?” depends on the time frame. For budgeting, I would pay attention to both accumulated increases and recent inflation, then compare them with what I actually buy.

These indexes cannot explain why prices changed or decide whether cooking is worth someone’s time. They do show why slowing inflation does not mean a return to earlier prices.

Data, methods and reproducibility

I use eight monthly BLS CPI-U series: food at home, food away from home, and six grocery categories. All are U.S. city averages without seasonal adjustment. I set January 2015 = 100 for cumulative comparisons and calculate annual inflation against the same month a year earlier, using 2014 observations for the first year’s rates. The code checks monthly coverage, duplicates and invalid values. Results depend on the chosen endpoints and do not describe a specific store or student budget.

Sources: BLS CPI, FRED’s food at home and food away from home series. All eight source links are recorded in results/series_dictionary.csv.

The GitHub repository contains the R code and replication README. Original CSVs and retrieval records are saved in data/raw, charts in figures, and calculated tables and R session information in results. Later renders reuse the saved inputs.
